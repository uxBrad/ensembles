# Research Methodology for Ensembles

How to gather the research needed to populate ensemble documents, how to synthesize that research into useful ensembles, and what to do with what you learn along the way.

This document is designed to be used in two ways. As a guide for researchers populating their own ensembles. And as documentation for the methodology behind the example ensembles in this repository, so you can evaluate how rigorously they were built.

If you're using this methodology for your own research, expect to adapt it. The interview questions are written for general use, but specific domains will benefit from domain-specific probes. The synthesis guidance is principles-based, but the specifics of how you apply them will depend on what you find.

## Contents

1. **Research plan template** - How to scope and run a research project that will produce well-grounded ensembles
2. **Interview script** - A reusable script for talking to people about the teams they work on
3. **Synthesis guide** - Principles for turning interview data into populated ensemble documents
4. **Methodology reflection template** - A structured prompt for capturing what you learn from doing this work

## Part 1: Research plan template

A research plan for ensemble work has different priorities than a typical UX research plan. You're not trying to understand individual users. You're trying to capture how a configuration of people works as a system. This changes what you ask, who you ask, and how you synthesize.

### Scoping decisions

Before you start, you need to make a few decisions about scope. These shape everything else.

**What problem is the research serving?**

Ensemble research can serve different goals. The most common are:

- Validating that the ensemble framework captures what matters about real teams in your domain
- Mapping the landscape of distinct ensemble configurations that exist in a market
- Populating specific ensemble documents you've already conceptually identified
- Some combination of the above

Be honest about which one you're really doing. A plan optimized for landscape mapping will under-serve document population, and vice versa. If you try to do all three at once, you'll do all three poorly.

**Which ensembles will you study?**

If you're doing landscape mapping, you're looking for variety. If you're populating specific documents, you're looking for depth on specific configurations.

For most projects, the sweet spot is somewhere in between. Identify two or three plausible ensemble configurations from your existing knowledge, and design the research to populate those while staying open to discovering configurations you hadn't anticipated.

**How many ensembles, and how many people per ensemble?**

A reasonable scope for a single researcher over 8 to 10 weeks is somewhere around 8 to 12 interviews, distributed across 3 to 5 ensembles. This gives you enough depth per ensemble to populate documents meaningfully, and enough breadth to notice patterns across configurations.

More interviews per ensemble (triangulating across multiple team members) is rigorous but slows recruiting. Fewer interviews per ensemble is faster but produces single-perspective documents that have known accuracy limits.

A compromise that works well for solo researchers: pick one or two ensembles where you can get 2 or 3 interviews each (triangulated), and a few additional ensembles where you can get one deep interview each (single-perspective). This gives you variety with at least some triangulated examples to anchor your synthesis.

**What's out of scope?**

Decide explicitly what you're not modeling in this round. For Echo's research, the choice was to model auditor ensembles only, not the product teams that receive their findings. Modeling the receiver side is a separate project that requires separate research.

This is worth being explicit about, because the temptation in the field will be to expand scope opportunistically. If a participant offers to introduce you to one of their product team contacts, the question is whether that fits this research or whether it's a different project.

### Recruiting

Recruiting for ensemble research is harder than recruiting for individual interviews. You're not just asking someone about themselves. You're asking them to describe their team's dynamics, which requires a level of psychological safety and willingness to be reflective that not everyone has.

A few things that help:

**Lead with the framing.** Tell potential participants you're studying how teams work, not how individuals work. People who find this interesting will self-select in.

**Promise anonymity for the team, not just the individual.** Team-level dynamics often involve specific people and specific tensions. Participants need to know that nothing will be attributable back to specific team members.

**Recruit from professional networks first.** Cold outreach is slow. People in your network are more likely to participate, and more likely to be reflective about team dynamics because trust is established.

**Look for variety, not representativeness.** You're not building statistical claims. You want to capture distinct configurations. Two interviews from very similar teams give you less information than two interviews from very different teams.

**Plan for 1.5x the interviews you want to do.** Some interviews will fall through. Some will turn out to be less useful than you hoped. Build buffer into your recruiting plan.

### Interview logistics

Some practical guidance for the interviews themselves:

**Allow 60 to 90 minutes.** Less than 60 and you can't get past surface answers. More than 90 and participants get tired. 75 minutes is a good default.

**Record if you can.** Synthesis is much harder from notes alone. Get explicit consent and record. If they say no, take detailed notes and accept that your synthesis will be less rigorous.

**Schedule two follow-ups.** A check-in question or two after the interview, by email, is often where the most interesting information surfaces. People think about the interview after it ends and remember things they wanted to say. Build in a follow-up touch and you'll capture some of that.

**Don't try to schedule too tightly.** Ensemble interviews require energy. Two per day is sustainable. Three per day is hard. Four per day is a recipe for shallow interviews.

### Timeline

For an 8 to 10 week project, a workable timeline:

- **Weeks 1 to 2:** Plan, recruit, draft script for your specific domain
- **Weeks 3 to 6:** Conduct interviews, with light synthesis between sessions
- **Weeks 7 to 8:** Deep synthesis, populate documents, identify gaps
- **Weeks 9 to 10:** Follow-up interviews to close key gaps, write up methodology reflection

The "light synthesis between sessions" matters. Don't wait until all interviews are done to start thinking about what you're learning. Each interview should sharpen your model of what to ask in the next one.

### Deliverables

For a typical ensemble research project, the deliverables are:

- One populated ensemble document per ensemble you studied
- A brief summary of what configurations you found (especially if they differed from what you expected)
- A list of gaps that future research should close
- A methodology reflection on what worked and what didn't

If your research is going into a product context (like populating Echo's customer ensembles), add a fifth deliverable: a product implications brief that translates what you learned into specific recommendations.

## Part 2: Interview script

This script is designed to surface the information you need to populate an ensemble document. It's organized around the schema's five sections, with deliberate flexibility for follow-up.

Use this as a starting point and adapt to your domain. The questions assume the participant is a member of a team. Adjust language if your participants describe themselves differently (a solo consultant is technically an "ensemble of one" but probably wouldn't use that language).

### Notes for interviewers

A few things to keep in mind throughout:

**Listen for specifics, not generalizations.** When a participant says "we're collaborative," that tells you nothing. When they say "if I push back on something, Diane will hear me out but Marcus will get defensive," that tells you everything. Probe relentlessly for specifics.

**Watch for the silences.** What people don't say is often more informative than what they do say. If a participant talks about everyone on the team except one person, that's signal. Ask about the person they didn't mention.

**Distinguish stated from observed.** People will tell you their team is collaborative, then describe a pattern that's clearly hierarchical. Both pieces of information are valuable. Capture both.

**Don't try to verify the framework.** Your job in the interview is to capture the team faithfully, not to confirm that the ensemble framework's schema applies. If the framework doesn't fit what you find, the framework needs to adapt. Note where things don't fit and worry about it during synthesis.

**Take notes on dynamics, not just content.** How does the participant talk about different team members? Do they soften when describing some and harden when describing others? Do they get more or less specific on certain topics? These are signal.

### Opening (5 minutes)

Goal: establish context and put the participant at ease. Don't try to extract information yet.

- "Thanks for talking with me. Before we get into the details, can you tell me a bit about your role and how long you've been in it?"
- "What does your team look like? How many people, what roles?"
- "How long have you all been working together?"

These should be quick. You're warming up, not interviewing.

### Section 1 - Identity and current state (10 minutes)

Goal: understand the team as a whole, what state they're in, and what their meta-properties are.

**Core questions:**

- "If you had to describe your team to someone who didn't know it, in just a few sentences, what would you say?"
- "What's the team's reputation in the organization? What do people outside the team think of you?"
- "What state is the team in right now? Energetic, exhausted, transitioning, stable, something else?"
- "What's the team good at that you don't think they get enough credit for?"
- "What's the team missing or systematically not doing well?"
- "What's happened in the last few months that's still shaping how the team operates?"

**Deviation notes for experienced interviewers:**

The "good at" and "missing" questions are where the Strengths and Blindspots section of the ensemble document comes from. Push hard here. Most people will give you safe answers first. Follow up with "what else?" and "what would the team itself disagree with you about there?" The most useful answers are often the ones the participant feels slightly uncomfortable giving.

The "state right now" question is doing important work. Teams have moods, and the mood shapes everything else. If they say "fine" or "normal," probe further. Ask about energy levels, recent frustrations, upcoming pressure.

### Section 2 - Roster (20 minutes)

Goal: understand each member of the team in the context of this team.

For each person on the team, ask:

- "Tell me about [person]. What's their role?"
- "How do they show up in team meetings? Do they speak up, hang back, dominate, listen?"
- "What are they known for? What do they reliably do?"
- "When is [person] at their best? What conditions make them more effective?"
- "When does [person] withdraw or get less effective?"
- "If you had to capture how [person] talks, what's something they say all the time?"

**Deviation notes:**

The "when at their best" and "when withdraws" questions are critical. They surface the modulation field in the schema, which is what makes the ensemble framework capture contextual instantiation rather than static traits.

If a participant describes someone in only positive or only negative terms, push for the other side. "What's something they're not great at?" or "What's something they actually do well even though it sounds like they frustrate you?" Real people are mixed, and one-sided portraits are signal that the participant isn't being fully reflective.

Watch the order in which the participant brings up people. They'll usually start with whoever they have the strongest opinions about, which is often the most central person on the team (positive or negative). Note this.

For each person, get at least one specific quote or phrase the person uses. Verbatim anchors are gold for ensemble documents.

### Section 3 - Topology (15 minutes)

Goal: understand how influence, trust, and decisions actually flow on the team.

**Core questions:**

- "Walk me through how a decision actually gets made on your team. Pick a recent one as an example."
- "Who has formal authority on the team? Who has real authority?"
- "Who tends to side with whom when there's a disagreement?"
- "Who pushes back on whom? Who can push back without consequences and who can't?"
- "Is there anyone on the team who doesn't speak up much? When and why?"
- "What's something you'd say in a one-on-one with a teammate that you'd never say in a team meeting?"

**Deviation notes:**

The last question is the most important and the hardest to get useful answers to. People are reluctant to admit they hold back in meetings. Frame it carefully. You can soften it by saying "this isn't about being secretive, it's about how all teams work, where some things just don't fit in group conversations."

The decision-making question often gets a textbook answer first ("we discuss as a team and reach consensus"). Push for the specific decision. "Okay, what was the actual decision? Who raised it first? Who had the strongest view? When did it actually get resolved?" The gap between the textbook answer and the actual answer is the gap between stated and actual decision-making mode in the schema.

If the participant struggles with the coalition question, give examples. "Like, if Marcus pushed for a particular approach, who would back him up first? And if Diane disagreed, who would she look to?"

### Section 4 - Repertoire (15 minutes)

Goal: surface recurring scenes the team plays out.

**Core questions:**

- "What's something that happens on your team that keeps happening, in roughly the same way?"
- "Walk me through what happens when [common scenario in your domain]. Who does what, in what order?"
- "Is there a pattern your team has where you can almost predict the outcome before it's resolved?"
- "Tell me about the last time the team had a meaningful disagreement. How did it play out?"
- "What does your team do when something goes wrong? Walk me through a specific time."

**Deviation notes:**

Repertoire questions are where the framework gets the most value, and they're the hardest questions to ask because participants don't naturally think this way. People remember individual events, not patterns. Your job is to surface patterns from individual events.

When the participant tells you about a specific event, ask: "Is that how it usually goes?" If yes, you've found a recurring scene. If no, ask what's different about that one.

The "predict the outcome" question is often where participants get the most reflective. Give them time to think. If they say "I'm not sure," try a more leading version: "Have you ever caught yourself thinking 'I know how this is going to end'? When?"

Scenes are most useful when they're domain-specific. For a software team, the scene might be "what happens when a feature requirement comes in late." For an accessibility team, it might be "what happens when a product team pushes back on a finding." Ask about the situations that come up most often in your participant's domain.

### Section 5 - Stimulus-response (10 minutes)

Goal: understand how the team metabolizes inputs from outside.

**Core questions:**

- "What kinds of things come at your team from outside? Requirements, requests, incidents, anything?"
- "Pick the most common one. What happens when something like that arrives? Who sees it first?"
- "What happens before the team formally talks about it? Are there DMs, hallway conversations, anything?"
- "Who tends to push back, and on what? Who tends to champion these things?"
- "Is there a difference between what you were asked for and what you actually delivered? Where does that difference come from?"

**Deviation notes:**

The "before the team formally talks about it" question is where pre-meeting layer information comes from. Most teams have a lot of invisible work that happens in DMs and informal conversations before anything reaches a formal meeting. Capturing that is important and easy to miss.

The "difference between asked and delivered" question surfaces the translation layer, which is often where the most actionable product insight lives. If your research is in service of a product, dig hard here.

### Closing (5 minutes)

Goal: capture anything you missed and set up follow-up.

- "Is there anything important about your team that we haven't talked about?"
- "If I came back to you in two weeks and you'd been thinking about our conversation, what's the thing you'd want to add?"
- "Would you be willing to answer a couple of follow-up questions by email if I think of things later?"

The "two weeks from now" question is doing real work. It plants a seed that the participant will keep thinking about, which makes the follow-up email more productive when you send it.

## Part 3: Synthesis guide

Synthesis is where research becomes ensembles. It's also where the framework's epistemic discipline gets tested. The temptation in synthesis is to fill in gaps with plausible content. Resist it.

The guidance in this section is principles-based. The synthesis skill described in `skills/synthesis-skill.md` operationalizes these principles, but the principles are what matter. If you understand them, you can do synthesis manually or with the skill, and the output will be defensible either way.

### Principle 1: Evidence first, claims second

When populating an ensemble document, work in this order:

1. Pull together the relevant evidence (interview quotes, observations, artifacts)
2. Identify what claims that evidence supports
3. Write the claim

Not the other way around. Don't decide what you want to say and then look for evidence. Start from what you have, and write claims that the evidence supports.

This sounds obvious but is hard in practice. Researchers have intuitions about what a team is like, and the intuitions are often correct, but they're not the same thing as evidence. If you write claims from intuition and look for evidence afterward, you'll find evidence for whatever you wanted to say. Confirmation bias is automatic in qualitative work. The discipline of evidence-first is the only thing that prevents it.

### Principle 2: Cite, even informally

Every populated field in an ensemble document should have an evidence note attached to it. Not formal citations. Just enough to trace claims back to their source.

A good format is `[INT-03]` or `[from team observation 2024-09-15]`. The point is that future-you, or anyone else reading the document, can find the underlying evidence in your notes.

If you can't cite a claim, the claim either needs to be marked as inference (see next principle) or removed.

### Principle 3: Distinguish evidence from inference

Some claims are directly supported by interview content. Others are reasonable inferences across multiple sources. Both are valid, but they should be marked differently.

Use a convention. For example:

- Claims with direct evidence: written normally, with citation
- Claims that are inference: marked with `[inferred]` or similar, with a note about what supports the inference

Inferences are often more valuable than direct claims, because they're where the synthesis work happens. But they have weaker grounding, and the document should be honest about that.

A good test: if a reader of your document later disagrees with a claim, can you point to specific evidence supporting it? If yes, the claim is well-grounded. If no, the claim is inference, and you should mark it so.

### Principle 4: Leave gaps explicit

When the schema has a field and your research doesn't support a confident claim, leave the field empty or mark it `[insufficient research]`. Do not fill it with plausible content.

This is the principle that gets violated most often. The document looks "incomplete" with empty fields. Stakeholders may push back on the gaps. There's pressure to produce a complete-looking artifact.

Resist this pressure. An ensemble document with explicit gaps is more useful than one with fabricated completeness. Gaps are research prompts. They tell you what to ask next. Fabricated content tells you nothing, and worse, looks authoritative when it isn't.

### Principle 5: Surface contradictions

When two pieces of evidence say different things about the same dynamic, don't average them. Surface the contradiction.

Sometimes contradictions are themselves the finding. A team where one member describes the dynamic one way and another member describes it another way is telling you something important about the team. That something is not "the truth lies in the middle." The discrepancy itself is data.

In the document, you might write something like: "[Member A's view: the team is consensus-driven. Member B's view: decisions are made by the team lead with input. Both perspectives appear genuine; the discrepancy may reflect different relationships with the team lead.]"

This is more useful than picking one or averaging.

### Principle 6: Cross-reference across interviews

For ensembles where you have multiple interviews (triangulated), cross-reference claims across them. If one member describes a coalition and another member implicitly confirms it through their own account, that's much stronger evidence than either alone.

Conversely, if one member describes something and another member never mentions it, that's worth noticing. Is the second member unaware of it? Does it not exist? Or is it a sensitive topic that one member is comfortable raising and another isn't?

Cross-referencing is what makes triangulated research valuable. Single-perspective documents can be useful, but they have known accuracy limits.

### Principle 7: Write the lightweight version first

The ensemble schema includes a 2-3 sentence prose paragraph at the top. Write this first, before populating the structured sections.

The lightweight version is a forcing function. If you can't capture the team's character in 2-3 sentences, you don't yet understand the team well enough to populate the full document. Doing the lightweight version first ensures that the structured sections are written from a coherent understanding, not from disconnected pieces.

It also gives you a touchstone. As you populate the structured sections, check back against the lightweight version. Does the detailed picture match the high-level one? If they diverge, one of them is wrong, and you need to figure out which.

### Principle 8: Note what surprised you

Throughout synthesis, keep a separate note of things that surprised you about the team. Things you expected to find and didn't. Things you didn't expect to find but did. Patterns that didn't fit the framework's schema.

These notes are valuable for two reasons. First, they're often where the most interesting findings live. The expected confirmations are useful for populating the document, but the surprises are where you might be discovering something the framework doesn't yet capture.

Second, they feed the methodology reflection (Part 4). You'll want to write up what you learned about the framework itself, and the surprises are the data for that.

### What good synthesis looks like

A well-synthesized ensemble document has these properties:

- Every claim is traceable to specific evidence
- Inferences are marked as inferences
- Gaps are explicit, not hidden
- Contradictions are surfaced
- The lightweight version and the structured sections tell the same story at different fidelities
- The document reads as a specific team, not a generic configuration
- Reading it produces predictions that the researcher would bet on, not vague impressions

If your synthesized document doesn't have these properties, it's not yet done. The temptation will be to ship it anyway. Resist that temptation; the document will be used for product decisions, and a half-baked document will produce half-baked decisions.

## Part 4: Methodology reflection template

After you finish a research project, spend a few hours filling in this template. The output becomes part of the framework's documentation, and helps future researchers understand what worked and what didn't.

### Project basics

- **What were you researching?**
- **What was the timeframe?**
- **How many interviews did you conduct?**
- **How many ensembles did you end up documenting?**

### What worked

- **What parts of the methodology worked as expected?**
- **What surprised you positively? Where did the framework or the script do more work than you expected?**
- **Which interview questions produced the most useful information?**

### What didn't work

- **What parts of the methodology fell short?**
- **Which interview questions consistently failed to produce useful information?**
- **Where did the schema feel like a poor fit for what you were finding?**
- **What did you have to adapt or improvise?**

### What you'd change

- **If you were doing this research again, what would you do differently?**
- **Are there questions you wish you had asked but didn't think of in time?**
- **Are there schema fields that should be added, removed, or modified?**
- **Would you change the recruiting approach, the timeline, the synthesis process?**

### What it cost

Honest accounting helps future researchers plan:

- **How many hours did the research take, in total?**
- **How many hours per ensemble document, on average?**
- **What was the most time-consuming part?**
- **What would have made it faster, if anything?**

### What you learned beyond the documents

- **What did you learn about the framework itself, separate from the specific ensembles?**
- **What did you learn about the domain you were researching?**
- **What new questions did the research open up?**

### How to use this section in the future

Aggregate methodology reflections from multiple researchers will eventually inform updates to the framework, the script, and the synthesis guide. If you're sharing your reflection, include it in any documentation update or pull request you make to the framework's repository.

If you keep your reflection private, it still serves you: when you do this work again, you'll be glad to have a record of what you learned.

## A note on adapting this methodology

Everything in this document is a starting point. The interview questions are written generally; your domain will need specific probes. The synthesis principles are universal, but how you apply them depends on what you find. The reflection template is a structure, not a constraint.

If you adapt the methodology in significant ways and the adaptations turn out to be improvements, consider contributing them back to the framework. The methodology gets better as more researchers use it across more domains, and the framework's documentation should reflect what's been learned collectively.

The accompanying synthesis skill specification (in `skills/synthesis-skill.md`) describes an AI assistant designed to help with the synthesis work. The skill is not a replacement for human judgment in synthesis; it's an assistant that helps with the mechanical parts (evidence tracking, gap identification, contradiction surfacing) while leaving the judgment work to the researcher.

If you build an implementation of that skill, test it against research where you already know what the right ensemble looks like, and make sure it produces appropriate gaps rather than confident fabrication. The framework's whole epistemic-honesty claim depends on the synthesis step staying disciplined.
